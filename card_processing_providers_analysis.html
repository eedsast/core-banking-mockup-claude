Card Processing Providers — Competitive Analysis

14 providers evaluated for European EMI card programmes · BIN sponsorship, white-label issuing and acquiring

Q1 — Licence & BIN sponsorship Does the provider hold its own EMI or bank licence and principal scheme membership, and will it act as BIN sponsor? Or is it processing only, requiring a separate sponsor?
Q2 — Product coverage White-label issuing and acquiring in one place? Business/corporate cards? Revolving or credit cards, as opposed to debit and prepaid only?
Three roles, often confused
How to read this analysis
A card programme needs all three roles filled — by one provider or by several
The roles
Sponsor · Processor · Manager
BIN sponsor holds the licence and scheme membership and carries regulatory liability. Issuer-processor runs authorisation, clearing and settlement. Programme manager owns the customer, the product and the operations.
Why it matters to you
Your platform is the fourth role
The core banking platform is none of these. It holds the ledger and the customer record and must integrate cleanly with whichever combination the customer has already chosen — which means a processor-abstraction layer, not a single hard-wired integration.
💡
Practical consequence: never quote a prospect on the assumption they will take your preferred card partner. Most arrive with an incumbent. Build one internal card API with adapters, certify two providers at launch, and treat the rest as a roadmap. Going direct to scheme membership typically costs 18+ months and substantial frozen collateral, versus roughly 8 weeks through a sponsor — which is why almost every EMI you sell to will be on a sponsored BIN.

Counterparty risk Cross-cutting
The failure mode nobody prices in
Applies to every sponsored programme in this document
What happened
Sponsor failure is not hypothetical
The Bank of Lithuania revoked UAB PayrNet's EMI licence over gross and systematic AML and payments-law violations, initiated bankruptcy proceedings and required funds to be returned. PayrNet was the European licensed entity behind Railsr and served a large number of downstream fintechs.
What it means for your design
Sponsor portability is a feature
When the sponsor goes, the programme goes: cards stopped working and client funds were frozen pending bankruptcy. The platforms that recovered fastest were the ones that could re-point to a second sponsor without rebuilding.
Risk factors to assess per sponsor
Balance-sheet strength and parentage — a Banking Circle or Société Générale subsidiary is a different risk class from a small independent EMI
Supervisory history: public enforcement actions, activity restrictions, licence conditions
Business model concentration — sponsors operating exclusively through intermediaries and agents have proved the most fragile
Prefund exposure: how much of your customer's money sits with the sponsor as security at any moment
Mitigations to build into the product
Processor and sponsor abstraction so a programme can be re-pointed without application changes
Data portability: card tokens, mandates, balances and transaction history exportable in a standard format on demand
Dual-sponsor capability for customers at scale — run two BINs in parallel
Prefund and settlement position visible in the customer's own ledger, not only in the sponsor's portal
💡
This is a sales argument, not just an engineering one. Every EMI founder who lived through 2023 understands sponsor risk viscerally. "Your programme survives your sponsor" is a sharper pitch than any feature comparison, and it is a direct consequence of architecture decisions you would make anyway.

Q1 — Licence & BIN sponsorship verdict

Seven of fourteen providers hold their own EMI or bank licence and will act as BIN sponsor. Only Enfuce, Paynetics and Edenred combine dual-perimeter EEA+UK licences with principal scheme membership — everyone else is single-scheme, single-perimeter, or both.
Single-scheme sponsorship is the norm, not the exception: Wallester is Visa-only, FinXP and Swan are Mastercard-only. Any customer wanting both schemes needs either Enfuce, Paynetics, or two relationships — which is itself an argument for your abstraction layer.
The processors and platform vendors (Paymentology, BPC, RS2) carry no licence and sponsor nothing, which makes them the cleanest complements: no product overlap, no regulatory entanglement, and the customer keeps the scheme relationship. It also means every customer on them still needs a sponsor, so your adapter must support the split "platform here, sponsorship there" shape.
Marqeta buying TransactPay in 2025 confirms the direction of travel: processors are acquiring EMIs to offer a single contract in Europe. Expect further consolidation and design so that a partner changing shape does not break your customers.

Q2 — Product coverage verdict

Business cards are near-universal; revolving and credit are not. EMI-only sponsors cannot lend, so "credit" at an EMI is usually deferred debit or a partner-funded facility. Genuine revolving credit requires a bank licence (Solaris) or a funding partner behind the programme.
Issuing and acquiring together is rare. Paynetics is the clearest single-counterparty answer; FinXP covers acceptance broadly. Everyone else is issuing-only, so marketplace and merchant propositions will need a second provider.
Certify two adapters at launch: one licensed sponsor (Enfuce or Paynetics) and one sponsor-agnostic processor or platform (RS2, or Paymentology where the customer already runs it). That pairing covers both the "bring everything" and the "bring your own sponsor" customer. Edenred is the third worth adding early, because its agent model reaches customers who will never hold a licence.
Three of the twelve — Swan, Treezor and Solaris — are competitors rather than partners. They sell the whole stack on their own licence. Your counter-positioning is unchanged in every case: the customer keeps its own licence, IBAN range, product control and economics.
